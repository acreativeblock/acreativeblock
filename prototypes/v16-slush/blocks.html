The six blocks

A block is a phase,
not an identity

Creative block isn't one thing — it's a symptom, and the cure depends on the cause. Being stuck doesn't mean you've lost your creativity or failed. It means something specific needs adjusting: direction, structure, recovery, or your relationship to the work. Here are the six patterns. Find yours.

01 · Directional

“I don't know what to make.”

You're not lazy, and you're not out of ideas — if anything you have too many. Every path looks possible, so committing to one feels like quietly killing the others. So you keep your options open, circle, and wait for a certainty that never quite arrives.

Signs it's your block
  • Not knowing what to make
  • Endless exploring without committing
  • Feeling scattered or purposeless
  • A lost connection to the work
  • Waiting for the “right” direction
Why it keeps coming back

Underneath the indecision is usually fear: as long as you don't choose, every version of you stays possible and none of them can fail. Committing means letting the other futures go — so the not-choosing isn't laziness, it's protection. It returns whenever the stakes of “getting it right” rise again.

The way through
Choose a direction, even imperfectlyAn imperfect destination beats standing still. You're picking something to test, not a life sentence.
Know which mode you're inExploring and committing are different jobs; confusing them keeps you stuck. Name which one you're in right now.
Reconnect with meaningDirection returns when the work stops being purely for approval. Ask what it's actually for.

If the real fear is choosing wrong and being judged for it, the Identity block is probably involved too.

02 · Structural

“It's a chaos in here.”

It's not that you don't want to work — it's that you can't find the edge to start at. Forty tabs, scattered files, a plan that only half-exists in your head. Every time you sit down you spend your energy just trying to see the shape of it, and there's nothing left for the actual making.

Signs it's your block
  • Chaotic organisation
  • Unclear briefs or goals
  • Endless revision loops
  • Scattered assets and files
  • Constant context-switching
  • A tool that fights how you think
Why it keeps coming back

The chaos is real — but it's also a place to hide. While everything's a mess, you never have to face the genuinely hard creative decision underneath. Reorganising feels like progress and keeps the scary part at arm's length, so the mess quietly regenerates every time you get close to the real work.

The way through
Run a friction auditList everything adding resistance — clutter, vague goals, bad workflow — and remove the friction, not yourself.
Use tools that fit your brainIf an app fights how you think, switch it. It's a fit problem, not a discipline failure.
Let structure protect creativityClear briefs and predictable routines cut decision fatigue and free energy for the real work.

If you keep reorganising instead of starting, the Execution block may be hiding underneath the chaos.

03 · Execution

“I know what to do — I just don't.”

You know exactly what to do. You've known for days. And still, somehow, you don't. It isn't discipline you're missing — the moment you go to begin, something quietly pulls the handbrake, and “later” floods you with relief. This is not laziness.

Signs it's your block
  • Perfectionism
  • Fear of failure
  • Comparison to others
  • Anxiety or overwhelm
  • Self-judgment
  • Burnout and learned helplessness
Why it keeps coming back

Avoiding the task works — it gives instant relief from the threat your nervous system senses. That relief is a reward, so the brain learns to do it again. And because you never actually start, you never get to prove the task was safe, so it stays charged. That's the loop — not a character flaw.

The way through
Move before you're readySmall action → feedback → adjust → repeat. Confidence follows action, not the other way around.
Lower the bar aggressivelyMake bad work on purpose. Aim to begin, not to be good.
Reframe the blockRead the stuck as incubation, not failure — it removes the second layer of panic.

If what you're really avoiding is being judged, the Identity block is likely in the mix.

04 · Identity

“What if I'm not good enough?”

Somewhere along the way the work stopped being something you make and became a verdict on who you are. So finishing feels dangerous — a finished thing can be judged, an unfinished one can't. You stall right near the end and call it perfectionism.

Signs it's your block
  • Perfectionism near the finish
  • Fear of publishing or sharing
  • Chronic comparison
  • Abandoning projects close to done
  • Overthinking how it'll be received
Why it keeps coming back

If your worth rides on the outcome, an unfinished piece is safer than a finished one — it can't be measured against the person you're afraid you might be. The block is doing a job: keeping your self-image intact. It returns whenever the work starts to feel like a test of you, rather than a thing you're making.

The way through
Separate self-worth from outputThe work is something you make — not a verdict on who you are. Say it out loud if you need to.
Normalise imperfect releaseShip the imperfect version. Finishing gets easier every time you do it.
Reduce catastrophic thinkingName the feared outcome and check the evidence. If it runs deep, a therapist is the right support.

If it also feels like you've lost direction, the Directional block may be feeding the fear.

05 · Environmental

“My life keeps killing my momentum.”

You keep blaming yourself — but look around first. The room, the noise, the notifications, the months without real rest. Your momentum is leaking out through your conditions before it ever reaches the work. This isn't a willpower problem.

Signs it's your block
  • Repetitive, draining routines
  • Overstimulation or screen overload
  • Isolation and lack of novelty
  • A space that doesn't support focus
  • Can't concentrate with notifications on
Why it keeps coming back

Willpower can't out-muscle a setup that's working against you. When your environment spends your attention and energy first, there's nothing left to push through with — so you read it as personal failure and try harder, which drains you faster. It returns because the conditions, not your character, never changed.

The way through
Change your contextNovel input disrupts stuck thinking — work elsewhere, walk, change the room. The mechanism is cognitive, not motivational.
Remove digital frictionClose the browser, silence notifications, work offline when you actually need to make. A closed browser is a valid tool.
Protect cognitive spaceRest, sleep and walking let insight surface. Forcing it under pressure makes the block worse.

If the drain is more exhaustion than surroundings, this often overlaps with Execution and burnout.

06 · Skill

“I don't know how to do the next bit.”

Sometimes the block isn't emotional at all. There's a specific thing the work needs that you haven't learned yet — and because the gap is vague, it feels like proof you're not good enough, instead of just a skill you don't have yet.

Signs it's your block
  • Stalling at technical stages
  • Over-consuming tutorials
  • Insecurity about your ability
  • Confusion about the next step
  • Impostor feelings
Why it keeps coming back

A vague gap is scarier than a named one. Not knowing exactly what you're missing lets it balloon into “maybe I'm just not good enough,” so you avoid looking at it directly — which keeps it vague, which keeps it scary. The loop only breaks once the gap gets specific.

The way through
Name the exact missing skillPinpoint what you genuinely can't do yet, separate from fear. A named gap shrinks.
Find the smallest learning pathLearn the one piece that unlocks the next step — not the whole field.
Get targeted inputA mentor, reference or worked example beats endless self-analysis when it's really a skill gap.

If naming the gap brings up “I'm not good enough,” the Identity block is layered in too.

Most people are a mix

You're probably more
than one

Hardly anyone is a single block. “I don't choose because I fear choosing wrong” is Directional + Identity. “It's so chaotic I avoid it” is Structural + Execution. That's normal — the job is to find the primary driver and work that first. Solving the secondary symptoms only wastes time.
