What we can say

A useful synthesis—not a finished scientific taxonomy.

Creative block is under-researched relative to how common it appears to be. There is no agreed scientific taxonomy of block types, and some of the strongest available evidence comes from writing, academic and artist samples rather than the full range of creative professions.

Our framework synthesises that research with established psychological ideas, coaching, creative practice and systems thinking. It helps us ask better questions, form testable hypotheses and choose a proportionate response. We present it as an evidence-informed practitioner method—not as a validated medical or psychological diagnosis.

Scope matters

Not everything difficult is a creative block.

A skill gap may need teaching. Exhaustion may need recovery. A health concern may require a qualified clinician. We work within scope and refer when another kind of support is more appropriate.

How claims are graded

Not all evidence has the same weight.

Direct evidence includes empirical creative-block studies and intervention research. Adjacent evidence comes from broader creativity, motivation, behaviour and problem-solving research. Practitioner evidence includes clinical literature, field practice and case material. Framework synthesis is where ACB organises those findings into usable domains and sequences.

We describe each claim at the strength the evidence supports. “Research shows” is not used where the more accurate phrase is “creativity research suggests” or “in our framework, we use”.

Research strands behind the method

A multidisciplinary evidence base.

The framework draws from creative-block research alongside work on intrinsic motivation, self-efficacy, mood and activation, incubation, fixation, rigid rules, avoidance, creative systems, environment and feedback.

What would change our mind?

The map is allowed to evolve.

We would revise the framework if stronger evidence showed that domains should be combined, separated or abandoned; if a distinction did not change intervention; or if practice repeatedly contradicted the working hypothesis.

That is the point of an evidence-informed method:it should support better observation and decisions, not protect its own labels.
