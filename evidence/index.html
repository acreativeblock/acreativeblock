The evidence

Creative blocks are common. Understanding what drives them is where useful work begins.

What we mean by a block

A stall, not a shortage of ideas.

Across the research, a creative block is a temporary inability to produce or continue creative work, not explained by a lack of skill, resources or commitment.8,20 The capacity is still there, but you cannot access it right now.

It isn't the same as resting or simply not having an idea yet—both of those can feel calm. A block comes with friction: the intention is there, the ability is usually there too, and the work still doesn't move. That gap between wanting to make something and being unable to is what we're describing on this page.

The research we draw on

The evidence points inward.

Set aside clinical conditions and external pressures like financial strain, and the evidence points inward: mechanisms inside the creative process itself do most of the damage to wellbeing.

Our framework brings together creative-block research, psychology, coaching, creative practice and systems thinking. Adjacent work on motivation, self-efficacy, mood, avoidance, fixation, incubation, environment and feedback helps us ask better questions—without pretending it directly validates our map.4,7,8,10,12

What the numbers say
78%

reported creative block. In one survey of 271 professionally active artists and writers, the large majority reported experiencing it.20

70%

experienced burnout in the prior year, against 53% in non-creative fields.18,16

~80%

live with severe intellectual self-doubt and imposter feelings.8

The symptom and the cause

The block is the visible part of a loop.

Burnout can drain the attention creative work needs. Perfectionism can make starting feel unsafe. When self-worth is tied to output, every decision can feel like a verdict. The stalled work can then add to the pressure that caused it—so the useful question is what started the loop.3,8,16,18,19

  1. 01 · Underneath

    Pressure builds

    Burnout, perfectionism or identity anxiety makes the work harder to enter.

  2. 02 · On the surface

    Work stalls

    The block is the part you can see: the intention is there, but the work does not move.

  3. 03 · What follows

    Pressure grows

    Avoidance and lost confidence make the next attempt feel harder.

How we organise it

Different lenses, one practical map.

The literature does not converge on a single taxonomy of creative block because researchers ask different questions. Cognitive work on design fixation studies why the mind sticks to a bad idea.12 Motivation research asks whether the reason to make the work still holds.7 Occupational psychology looks at burnout and effort–reward imbalance.16,19 Incubation research looks at what happens when you stop pushing and let the problem rest.10 Each lens explains a different part of the experience; none was built to be picked up at a desk on a Tuesday morning.

So we built the 7+1 map: seven domains where creative work gets stuck, plus one look-alike—a skill gap that is not a block at all. It does not claim to be the final taxonomy. It is a practical way to locate what needs attention next.

Why it matters

Same block, different driver, different fix.

Two people can arrive with the identical stuck feeling and need opposite first moves—because the domain underneath is different. The block is a symptom; naming the right domain keeps us from treating the surface while the real driver stays untouched.

What we can—and cannot—claim

A useful practitioner method, not a clinical diagnosis.

What we can do

Organise observation, form working hypotheses, and match a proportionate response to the likely mechanism.

What we do not claim

That the framework is a validated diagnostic instrument, that its domains are the final taxonomy, or that one label determines treatment.

How we hold it

As a map that earns its place by being useful—revised as evidence, supervision and practice teach us more.
